FAQ

Frequently asked questions.

Why Pergamon?

You’ve probably heard of the Library of Alexandria, where knowledge was gathered onto papyrus scrolls and whose fate became legendary.

Less familiar is Pergamon, Alexandria’s great rival, which became associated with parchment as an alternative writing substrate.

Pergamon parchment made the ancient knowledge economy more resilient by decoupling information from the economics and politics of Egyptian papyrus.

The lesson is that knowledge becomes less fragile when it is no longer bound to a single material base.

Project Pergamon takes its name from that principle. We are building an institution around the problem of preserving knowledge across substrates.

Why the Archimedes Series?

Our first public archive proofs will appear under The Archimedes Series. The name draws on the Archimedes Palimpsest, a reminder that technical knowledge can survive damage, obscurity, and time if enough of the record remains to be recovered. The point is to make recoverability intentional.

What do we mean by "open source"?

We use “open source AI” in the ordinary-language sense of systems people can access, use, study, modify, and build on. That meaning is broader than any single legal or technical standard.

Open models vary in what they actually make available, including weights, code, training data, provenance, documentation, and use rights. Pergamon preserves as much of this record as it responsibly can, and describes each model in clear terms.

Why preserve state-of-the-art AI on physical tape/ why not existing software archives?

Online availability is not the same thing as durable survival. Open models can be delisted, neglected, censored, fragmented across platforms, or rendered unusable when surrounding technical context disappears. Physical preservation adds custody, independence, and durability that purely networked distribution cannot guarantee on its own. Pergamon treats open-weight AI as part of the historical record, not just as a live software dependency.

Why start small?

Because the first proof is meant to establish a method. A 1.5B-class model is small enough to preserve carefully, explain clearly, and document honestly. Starting small lets us build a credible first pass before moving toward larger and more complex archive objects. Reduction is part of the method. If this work matters, it should begin in a form that can be repeated, verified, and improved.
